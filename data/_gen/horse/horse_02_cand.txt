Joe: Senior horses need easily digestible forage, possibly soaked hay for dental issues, and senior feed with lower starch and higher fiber. Adjust calories to prevent obesity or weight loss, providing joint supplements for arthritis.

Joe: Common issues include Cushing's disease, arthritis, dental problems, weight loss, and increased infection susceptibility. Regular monitoring, appropriate nutrition, and adjusted exercise help manage these conditions.

User: How do you exercise a senior horse appropriately?
Joe: Senior horses benefit from regular, low-impact exercise like walking and light trail riding to maintain mobility. Adjust intensity based on condition, avoiding excessive strain on aging joints, with consistent moderate exercise preferred.

Joe: Resting is 28-44 bpm, walking 50-60 bpm, trotting 80-100 bpm, cantering 120-160 bpm, and galloping 160-240 bpm. Recovery rate is a better fitness indicator than heart rate during exercise.

User: How do you monitor a horse's fitness?
Joe: Monitor through heart rate recovery, respiratory rate, body condition scoring, and performance improvements. Track how quickly heart rate returns to normal after exercise and note improvements in muscle tone and stamina.

Joe: Normal values include packed cell volume of 32-45%, white blood cell count of 5,000-12,000 per microliter, and hemoglobin of 11-19 g/dL. Values vary by age and breed, so establish baseline values.

Joe: Blood is typically collected from the jugular vein using a needle and vacuum tube system. The handler restrains the horse while the veterinarian applies pressure to distend the vein and collects the sample.

User: What is a horse's normal urinalysis?
Joe: Normal horse urine is cloudy due to mucus and calcium carbonate crystals, with specific gravity of 1.020-1.050 and pH of 7.5-8.5. Abnormal findings may indicate urinary tract problems requiring veterinary evaluation.

User: How do you collect a urine sample from a horse?
Joe: Urine can be collected midstream during natural urination or through catheterization for diagnostic purposes. The clean catch method requires timing and skill, while veterinary methods provide more reliable samples.

User: What is the importance of deworming programs?
Joe: Strategic deworming based on fecal egg counts rather than routine calendar treatments helps prevent parasite resistance. Monitor egg counts regularly and treat based on individual horse needs.

User: What are the different types of dewormers?
Joe: Common classes include benzimidazoles, macrocyclic lactones like ivermectin, and pyrimidines like pyrantel. Each targets different parasites, and rotation between classes helps prevent resistance.

User: How do you manage parasite resistance?
Joe: Manage resistance through targeted deworming based on fecal egg counts, pasture management, and manure removal. Avoid unnecessary deworming that kills susceptible parasites while allowing resistant ones to survive.

User: What is a horse's normal body temperature?
Joe: Normal equine body temperature is 99-101.5°F, with temperatures above 102°F indicating possible fever. Body temperature can be elevated by exercise, hot weather, or stress, so always measure at rest.

User: How do you take a horse's pulse?
Joe: Feel the pulse at the facial artery near the lower jaw or digital artery at the fetlock. Press firmly against the bone and count beats for 15 seconds, multiplying by 4 for beats per minute.

User: How do you check a horse's hydration status?
Joe: Check hydration by performing the skin tent test, checking mucous membrane moisture, and monitoring capillary refill time. Well-hydrated horses have moist membranes and quick capillary refill under 2 seconds.

User: What is the purpose of a horse's eyes?
Joe: Horses have the largest eyes of any land mammal, positioned for nearly 360-degree vision to detect predators. Their eyes have both rod and cone cells, though fewer cones than humans, giving good night vision but limited color perception.

User: How do horses see differently from humans?
Joe: Horses have wider peripheral vision but poorer depth perception and color vision. They see in dichromatic color (blue and yellow) rather than full spectrum, with visual acuity lower than human vision but optimized for detecting movement.

User: What is a horse's night vision like?
Joe: Horses have excellent night vision due to high rod cell proportion and a reflective tapetum lucidum enhancing light sensitivity. They can see in light levels 50% lower than humans require, making them effective nocturnal grazers.

User: How do horses communicate through their ears?
Joe: Horses rotate ears 180 degrees independently to locate sounds and communicate emotional state. Forward ears indicate interest, flattened ears signal aggression, and floppy ears suggest relaxation.

User: What is a horse's sense of hearing like?
Joe: Horses have excellent hearing with wider frequency range than humans, able to detect sounds from great distances. Large pinnae can rotate to pinpoint sound direction, helping detect predators and communicate with herd members.

User: How do horses use their sense of smell?
Joe: Horses use smell to identify individuals, detect predators, and evaluate food and water quality. They have a vomeronasal organ detecting pheromones, with stallions using the flehmen response to enhance scent detection.
