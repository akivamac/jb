User: How often should a farrier visit?
Joe: Most horses need the farrier every 6 to 8 weeks, though some grow hoof faster and need 5-week intervals. The farrier trims the hoof to balance and replaces or resets shoes as needed.
User: What happens if I skip a farrier visit?
Joe: Overgrown hooves crack, chip, and distort, shifting the horse's weight onto unhealthy structures. Long-term neglect causes lameness, joint strain, and hoof deformities that take months to correct.

User: What does a farrier do during a trim?
Joe: The farrier cleans the hoof, cuts the excess wall, trims the sole and frog to healthy levels, and balances the foot to match the horse's conformation. A good trim leaves the hoof flat and level so the horse stands square.
User: How do I know if my farrier did a good job?
Joe: The horse should stand level on both front feet and both hind feet, and the trimmed hoof should show even wear patterns. If the horse is suddenly lame or foot-sore after a trim, the farrier may have taken too much off one area.

User: What is the difference between hot shoeing and cold shoeing?
Joe: Hot shoeing heats the metal shoe so the farrier can shape it precisely to the hoof, creating a custom fit. Cold shoeing uses pre-shaped shoes adjusted with a hammer and file, which is faster but less exact.
User: Is hot shoeing better for my horse?
Joe: Hot shoeing allows a more precise fit because the farrier sees exactly where the shoe contacts the hoof. It also seals the trimmed hoof wall and reduces the risk of white line separation, but it costs more and takes longer.

User: What is a bar shoe used for?
Joe: A bar shoe has a metal bar across the heels and is used to support the frog and heel area. It is often prescribed for navicular syndrome, quarter cracks, or after an abscess to relieve pressure on the back half of the hoof.
User: How long does a horse wear a bar shoe?
Joe: Bar shoes are typically used for one to three shoeing cycles, depending on the injury or condition. Once the hoof has healed or the farrier sees improvement, the horse returns to a standard shoe.

User: What are hoof boots and when are they used?
Joe: Hoof boots are removable rubber or synthetic boots that protect the hoof on rough terrain. They are used for barefoot horses on rocky trails, for horses with thin soles, or to protect a hoof that lost a shoe during a ride.
User: Can a horse wear hoof boots full-time?
Joe: Hoof boots are designed for temporary wear, not 24/7 use, because they trap moisture and can cause skin irritation. They work best for specific activities like trail riding or turnout on abrasive ground.

User: What is a heart bar shoe?
Joe: A heart bar shoe is a special farriery shoe with a curved extension that supports the frog and coffin bone. It is used for horses with laminitis or founder to relieve pressure on the damaged hoof wall.
User: Does a heart bar shoe cure laminitis?
Joe: A heart bar shoe does not cure laminitis but supports the coffin bone and reduces pain during recovery. It must be fitted precisely by an experienced farrier, and the underlying metabolic condition must also be managed.

User: Why does my horse keep losing shoes?
Joe: Frequent shoe loss can mean the hoof wall is weak or crumbly, the farrier's nails are placed too shallow, or the horse is overreaching and pulling them off. A farrier can use nail placement, clips, or even glue-on shoes for horses that pull shoes.

Joe: Glue-on shoes attach to the hoof wall with adhesive rather than nails. They are useful for horses with weak or thin hoof walls that cannot hold nails, or for therapeutic purposes where nails might compromise the hoof.

User: How do I fix a bridging saddle?

User: What is the tree of a saddle?
Joe: The tree is the internal frame of the saddle, traditionally made of wood but now often synthetic or spring steel. It determines the saddle's width, balance, and how it distributes the rider's weight.

User: What is a gullet on a saddle?
Joe: The gullet is the channel under the saddle's pommel that runs along the horse's spine. It must be wide enough to clear the withers and spine with at least two to three fingers of space.
User: What happens if the gullet is too narrow?
Joe: A narrow gullet pinches the withers, causing pain, muscle atrophy, and head tossing or bucking under saddle. Over time it can damage the withers' ligaments and create permanent white hair patches from pressure.

User: How do I know if my saddle is too wide?
Joe: A saddle that is too wide sits low on the horse's back, often rocking side to side, and the pommel may touch the withers. The rider feels unstable, and the horse may develop sore spots along the spine.
User: Can a too-wide saddle be fixed?
Joe: A fitter can add flocking or a thicker pad to take up the space, but a saddle that is drastically too wide may never fit well. In that case, a different saddle with a narrower tree is the better solution.

Joe: Common bits include the snaffle, curb, Pelham, and Kimberwicke, each applying pressure to different parts of the mouth and poll. Snaffles are the mildest and most common for general riding.

Joe: Full-cheek snaffles are excellent for green horses that lean on one rein or try to evade the bit sideways. The cheek pieces give subtle turning aids without harsh mouth pressure.

Joe: Mullen mouth bits are common in endurance riding and for horses with sensitive mouths that object to jointed bits. They are also used in some double bridles as the weymouth mouthpiece.

Joe: The double bridle allows the rider to refine the connection and collect the horse more subtly. It is a tool for upper-level dressage, not for fixing training problems, and should only be used under instruction.

User: What is a normal digital pulse?
Joe: A normal digital pulse is soft and difficult to detect. A strong, throbbing pulse that you can feel easily is abnormal and suggests the hoof is inflamed, requiring an immediate vet call.
